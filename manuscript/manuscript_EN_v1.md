## Title

**Auditing the testability of public omics resources: five failure modes that manufacture both false positives and false nulls in gene-set based disease studies**

**Running title**: A testability audit ladder for public omics data

**Authors**: Yike Yuan¹ (yyk20020329@126.com), Hongci Chen²\* (chenhongci@hbhtcm.com)

¹ Hubei University of Chinese Medicine, Wuhan, Hubei, China — ORCID 0009-0009-8391-4134
² Hubei Provincial Hospital of Traditional Chinese Medicine, Wuhan, Hubei, China — ORCID 0009-0000-3335-8998

\* Corresponding author.

## Abstract

**Motivation.** A large and growing literature intersects a curated gene set with disease differentially expressed (DE) genes, trains a classifier, and reports the surviving genes as biomarkers. Whether the underlying public data could in principle have returned a negative result is almost never asked. We treat that omission as a measurable methodological object and build a five-layer audit ladder that answers it at negligible cost.

**Results.** We applied the ladder to a concrete claim — that stress-granule-related genes (SGRG) constitute a molecular signature of ulcerative colitis (UC) — across five independent data levels. (i) *Bulk discovery.* In the discovery cohort (GSE38713; 43 colonic biopsies; 15 inflamed UC vs 13 controls) 44 of 97 measured SGRG genes reached FDR < 0.05, and the 97-gene panel discriminated disease with AUC = 0.936. Both were explained by data structure rather than biology: batch (scan date) was strongly confounded with disease (Cramér's V = 0.71, χ² = 21.8, df = 6, p = 1.3 × 10⁻³), a batch-only model reached AUC = 0.772, the panel's AUC sat at the 84.5th percentile of 200 size-matched random gene sets (median 0.910), and centring batch collapsed it to 0.580. In an independent cohort (GSE11223; 61 inflamed UC vs 62 normal) only 7 of the discovery genes replicated at FDR < 0.05, and cross-cohort effect sizes correlated weakly (Pearson r = 0.213, p = 0.081). (ii) *Database-wide landscape.* Across 160 public UC cohorts carrying machine-readable curation metadata, 40 (25%) were flagged as batch artifacts and a further 56 (35%) lacked the information needed to judge; in the 61 cohorts where both axes were measurable, batch-only separability of disease was a median of 0.828 in artifact-flagged cohorts versus 0.568 in batch-corrected ones. (iii) *Summary-statistic Mendelian randomisation.* Nine gene–tissue pairs were nominally significant, but within cis regions the effective number of independent instruments was ~1 because distance pruning had been used instead of LD clumping (e.g. ATXN2L: 10 instruments, effective 1.23, p 2.8 × 10⁻²⁸ → 6.7 × 10⁻⁶; OGFOD1: 10 instruments, effective 1.09, p 1.7 × 10⁻⁷ → 0.118). Colocalisation independently rejected all nine (maximum PP.H4 = 0.108; 0 pairs above 0.5). (iv) *Single-cell atlases.* In the largest UC dataset, 94.4% of "control" cells were re-used from external donors; restricting to the study's own two controls reduced the UC-to-control sequencing-depth ratio from 4.35× to 1.04×. Under that same-source contrast, all five inflammation-positive-control programmes were non-significant across all 23 tested cell types (0/23), and a within-donor inflamed-versus-non-inflamed contrast (4 pairs) admitted a minimum attainable p of 0.125. A whole-database enumeration through four gates (mRNA assay, contains UC, ≥6 donor pairs, in vivo tissue) returned only 2 qualifying resources out of 397 CELLxGENE collections and 521 GEO series. The same metadata, read across the whole library, shows the problem is structural: 41.3% of all 292.8M deposited cells are not primary to the dataset reporting them, the median dataset that carries a case/control contrast is 94% re-used cells, and in 89.3% of contrast-bearing collections the two arms cannot be separated from metadata at all. (v) *A powered bulk design.* The one qualifying resource that had never been analysed was GSE133060 — 8 UC patients each sampled twice (inflamed vs non-inflamed mucosa). Unlike every single-cell contrast we tested, it was a *powered* design: five Hallmark inflammation programmes rose in 8/8 patients (Δz 0.27–0.82, p = 0.0078, the attainable floor), whereas in the single-cell data a known-positive control had never recovered significance. Even here, though, the SGRG panel's nominal p = 0.016 carried no information: half of 500 expression-matched random gene sets were also nominally significant. Calibrated against that null the panel ranked 1/501 (one-sided empirical p = 0.002; two-sided 0.15; 0.006 after removing the global axis), with an effect size (Δz = 0.21) about a quarter that of the strongest positive control. The result is a weak, direction-consistent positive that required calibration to see — not the strong signature the original template would have reported. (vi) *Generalisation to independent published claims.* To test that the ladder is not specific to SGRG × UC, we applied it to two further published gene-set × disease claims, each frozen in a registered table before any analysis. For a ferroptosis × hepatocellular carcinoma claim (reported AUC = 1.000), the pipeline reproduced (in-sample AUC 0.9991) but the panel was indistinguishable from random gene sets of the same size (7-fold-CV empirical p = 0.611), the perfect performance was carried by a single global axis (removing it: 0.9991 → 0.757), and the paper's own discovery cohort contained a 2.29× pseudoreplication (54 samples from 18 patients). For a pyroptosis × Parkinson's disease claim (reported AUC = 0.823), the reported performance could not be recovered from public curated data under the published fixed weights (0.6432; validation 0.6250), although the direction replicated (5/5 cohorts); the score was dominated by cohort membership (ρ = −0.691 pooled vs −0.164 within), and the single-cell "validation" arm was fully nested (5/5 cases from one dataset; 77.8% of controls from case-free datasets). In both cases the panel was indistinguishable from a random set of the same size — one reproducible but non-specific, the other not recoverable — showing that these failure modes are properties of designs and data, not of a particular gene set.

**Conclusions.** Every null we obtained is a measurable property of the available data, not evidence about the biology of the disease. The failures are not specific to stress granules: the same five layers recurred when the ladder was applied to two further published claims in unrelated diseases (ferroptosis × hepatocellular carcinoma and pyroptosis × Parkinson's disease, §2.8), and they arise from control provenance, batch–disease nesting, depth–score confounding, uncorrected permutation tests, and a hard power floor — five layers that can be checked from metadata that is already public. We propose the ladder as a routine reporting requirement for gene-set × disease studies, and we report the outcome of the single resource that met its testability condition — a calibrated weak positive whose effect size was a quarter of a known-inflammatory programme.

**Keywords**: reproducibility; data re-use; batch effects; sequencing depth; Mendelian randomisation; power analysis; stress granules; ulcerative colitis

## 1. Introduction

Curated gene sets are now a standard input to disease bioinformatics. A common template intersects a set of genes — often assembled from a Gene Ontology term or a published proteomic census — with the differentially expressed (DE) genes of a disease cohort, prioritises the intersection with a supervised learner, and reports the surviving genes as biomarkers, usually accompanied by a nomogram, an enrichment analysis, and a drug-prediction network. The template is applied at scale: it has produced biomarker panels for periodontitis [1] and for chronic rhinosinusitis with nasal polyps [2], among many others.

Reports built on this template share a structural blind spot. They establish that a panel *is* associated with disease in a given dataset; they do not establish that the dataset could have returned a negative result. Statistical significance and discriminative performance are both produced by the data, so a panel can be "validated" by a cohort whose case and control arms differ in ways that have nothing to do with the disease. When the same template is applied to a second disease with less favourable data, the resulting null is then read as a biological contrast — as though the gene set were more fundamentally involved in the well-sampled disease.

Stress granules (SGs) provide a clean instance of this reading. SGs are membraneless ribonucleoprotein condensates that assemble when translation initiation stalls, and their dysregulation is implicated in a disease spectrum far wider than oncology: dominant mutations in core components such as TDP-43 and FUS cause familial amyotrophic lateral sclerosis and frontotemporal dementia [3,4]; SGs are a frontline antiviral defence, to the extent that SARS-CoV-2 encodes a dedicated antagonist [5,6]; and altered SG dynamics are reported in inflammatory, cardiovascular, hepatic and metabolic conditions [5,6,7]. A proteomic census of mammalian SG cores linked 75 stress granule components to 153 Mendelian diseases [8]. Yet transcriptomic studies using stress-granule-related gene (SGRG) sets are overwhelmingly concentrated in cancer, which invites the inference that SGs matter more in malignancy. We argue that this distribution is better explained by data availability and confounding structure — tumour tissue is the most densely deposited material in public repositories, the tumour microenvironment is itself a potent SG-inducing stressor, and SGRG sets overlap heavily with proliferation programmes and, by construction, with the translation-initiation and mRNP-remodelling machinery that defines the granules themselves [8,9] — than by biology. Whether the SGRG set is a stable measurement instrument at all is itself unresolved: re-analysis of published SG transcriptomes shows that profiles from differential centrifugation are dominated by an RNA-length-dependent physical bias and agree poorly with proximity labelling [10].

We therefore take the SGRG × ulcerative colitis (UC) claim as a worked example and ask, at five independent data levels, whether a null result is informative. We do not add a new positive claim. Instead we build a five-layer audit ladder — control provenance, batch–disease nesting, depth–score confounding, test calibration, and power floor — that converts "the panel was not significant" into "the data could not have shown significance, and here is the measurement that demonstrates it". Each layer is answerable from metadata that is already public, and each was decisive here.

## 2. Results

### 2.0 The audit ladder

The ladder has five rungs, ordered by the cost of the check (Box 1). Rung 1 asks where the control cells or samples came from. Rung 2 asks whether the technical axis is nested within the disease axis. Rung 3 asks whether the score being tested is a function of sequencing depth or other technical load. Rung 4 asks whether the test's null distribution is valid for the observed design. Rung 5 asks whether the design could have detected a known-positive effect at all. A study passes a rung by reporting the corresponding quantity; it fails by not being able to. Aggregated over a resource collection, the ladder becomes a feasibility filter: it tells you in advance which datasets can support the claim.

**Box 1. The five-rung testability audit ladder**

| Rung | Question | Quantity to report | Failure signature |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Where did the controls come from? | fraction of control cells/samples that are primary to the study (`is_primary_data`, `donor_id`) | controls largely re-used from external donors |
| 2 | Is the technical axis nested in the disease axis? | Cramér's V / χ² between batch and group; batch-only classifier AUC | batch-only AUC ≈ or > panel AUC |
| 3 | Is the score a function of technical load? | ρ(score, nUMI) within cell type; R²(depth ~ disease); depth ratio case/control | strong score–depth correlation with disease-aligned depth |
| 4 | Is the null distribution valid? | random-gene-set null for the *same* test; permutation validity check | more "significant" cell types under permutation than expected |
| 5 | Could the design have detected anything? | positive-control (known pathway) result; minimum attainable p | known-positive controls all non-significant |

### 2.1 Rung 2 at scale: batch–disease nesting in public UC cohorts

**Discovery cohort.** We assembled the SGRG panel from GO:0010494 ∪ GO:0035061 (156 QuickGO symbols → 104 HGNC-approved; 97 measured on the discovery array). In GSE38713 (43 human colonic mucosal biopsies; UC active inflamed n = 15 vs control n = 13), 44 of 97 genes reached FDR < 0.05 (24 up-regulated, 20 down-regulated) — an apparently clean directional result.

The structure of the cohort contradicts that reading. Batch (scan date) and disease were strongly confounded: Cramér's V = 0.71 (χ² = 21.8, df = 6, p = 1.3 × 10⁻³). Three of seven batches contained no controls at all, and one batch contained 7 of the 13 controls. A classifier trained on batch alone discriminated disease with AUC = 0.772, and the first principal component of the expression matrix — which explained 17.8% of variance — separated disease with AUC = 0.918. Critically, the panel's own discriminative performance was not distinguishable from chance for a set of that size: the 97-gene panel reached AUC = 0.936, but 200 size-matched random gene sets drawn from the same 18,630-gene pool had a median AUC of 0.910, placing the panel at the 84.5th percentile (Fig. 3a). A panel drawn at random would not have looked very different.

Removing the technical axis with batch centring collapsed the panel to AUC = 0.580 — below the random-set median of 0.505 only marginally, and at the 98.5th percentile of that null. Because batch centring in a design this confounded necessarily removes confounded disease signal as well, 0.580 is a lower bound rather than an estimate; the point is that the panel's apparent performance is not separable from the batch structure.

**Independent cohort.** In GSE11223 (61 inflamed UC vs 62 normal, colonic epithelial biopsies), 69 of the panel's genes were measured and 33 reached FDR < 0.05. Only 7 of the discovery genes replicated with the same direction at FDR < 0.05 (CPSF6, LARP4B, MBNL1, MCRIP2, RBM4, RNF135, YTHDF3), all down-regulated, with small effects (log₂FC −0.043 to −0.087). Cross-cohort effect sizes correlated weakly (Pearson r = 0.213, p = 0.081) and sign agreement was 57.4%. The asymmetry is itself informative: down-regulated discovery genes replicated far more often than up-regulated ones, consistent with the up-regulated component being driven by technical rather than disease variation.

**Database-wide.** The same confound is not a property of one cohort. Querying the Gemma database for UC transcriptomic cohorts returned 160 datasets with curation metadata. Of these, 40 (25%) carried an explicit batch-artifact annotation, 56 (35%) lacked the information required to judge, 18 (11%) were annotated as batch-corrected, and 46 (29%) as free of batch effects — i.e. 96 of 160 (60%) were problematic or unjudgeable. In the 61 cohorts where both a batch-separability and a disease-separability score could be computed on the same component structure, the batch-only AUC for disease was a median of 0.828 in artifact-flagged cohorts versus 0.568 in batch-corrected cohorts (median difference +0.228, Mann–Whitney p = 1.4 × 10⁻⁵); the batch-minus-biological AUC difference was +0.023 in artifact-flagged cohorts and −0.241 in batch-corrected ones. The database's own quality score (GEEQ) tracked this: it correlated negatively with measured batch separability (Spearman ρ = −0.346, p = 0.005).

We note explicitly what this analysis is and is not. It is an *audit of the same platform's curation output using that platform's own component summary*; it is therefore a consistency check, not an independent validation, and the platform's curation paper already states that batch factors are almost always associated with one of the first three principal components. Our contribution here is not to "discover" that public UC cohorts are batch-affected, but to quantify how often the batch axis is not merely present but *aligns with the disease axis*, and to show that this alignment is what a gene-set classifier is measuring.

### 2.2 Rung 3 and 5: depth confounding and the power floor in single-cell atlases

We next asked whether the same claim could be tested at cellular resolution, using four public UC mucosal scRNA-seq datasets (ds1: 46,700 cells, 18 donors, 6 UC vs 6 control; ds2: 11,720 myeloid cells, 81 donors; ds3: 87,621 large-intestine cells, 59 donors; ds4: 320,979 cells, 22 donors).

**Naive contrast manufactures a signal.** In ds4, the naive contrast (all cells labelled UC vs all cells labelled normal) produced 13 of 26 cell types significant for the SGRG score, and the UC-to-control depth ratio was 1.77× at donor level and 4.35× at cell level. Sequencing depth and the SGRG score were tightly coupled within cell types (median ρ = 0.81), and depth alone separated disease with AUC = 0.911 — the score was largely reporting how deeply each arm had been sequenced.

**Rung 1 breaks the contrast.** The dataset's "normal" arm was not generated by the study. Only 5.6% of its control cells were primary to the dataset (`is_primary_data` = true); the remainder were re-used from external donors (94.4%). Restricting the contrast to the study's own two controls reduced the cell-level depth ratio from 4.35× to 1.04× — the disease-aligned depth difference was an artefact of mixing in external, lower-depth control data (Fig. 7). The same check on the other datasets showed that their control pools were likewise dominated by cells from studies that contained no cases (ds2 98.5%, ds3 80.7%).

**Rung 4 invalidates the test.** Down-sampling all cells to a common 1,000 UMI removed 12 of the 13 significant cell types, and — the crucial control — a set of 200 expression-stratified random gene sets behaved identically, so the disappearance cannot be attributed to rarefaction "washing out" real signal (Fig. 5).

**Rung 5 shows the design had no power.** The decisive evidence is the positive control. Under the same-source contrast (study's own controls only), all five Hallmark inflammation programmes — hypoxia, inflammatory response, IFN-γ response, TNF-α signalling via NF-κB, and unfolded protein response — were non-significant in **all 23** tested cell types (0/23), while the SGRG panel was significant in 1 of 23. A design in which programmes that *must* differ between inflamed and non-inflamed mucosa fail to register cannot be used to argue that a specific panel does not differ. A within-donor inflamed-versus-non-inflamed contrast, which controls provenance exactly, was available for only 4 donor pairs, where the minimum attainable two-sided p is 2/2⁴ = 0.125 — a hard floor above any conventional threshold (Fig. 8).

**Rung 4 quantitatively: permutation is anti-conservative here.** Under the unpaired Mann–Whitney test, random gene sets produced a median of 6 "significant" cell types when 1.3 were expected — a 4.6-fold inflation. Under the permutation test the same null produced a median of 11 (8.5-fold inflation). The mechanism is non-exchangeability: UC samples contained more cells at greater depth, so UC pseudobulk values had roughly half the variance of control pseudobulk values (median SD ratio 0.49), and a permutation test that assumes exchangeability is anti-conservative. Against this calibrated null the SGRG panel was unremarkable: 5 significant cell types under the unpaired test (empirical p = 0.86) and 13 under permutation (empirical p = 0.40) (Fig. 6). Any report of "N significant cell types" without such a calibrated null is uninterpretable.

### 2.3 Rung 2 in summary statistics: distance pruning is not LD clumping

The same failure appears one layer below expression, in causal inference. We ran two-sample cis-Mendelian randomisation (MR) for SGRG genes against UC using GTEx v8 cis-eQTLs and the de Lange 2017 UC GWAS [11], and obtained 9 nominally significant gene–tissue pairs out of 50 tested.

Repeating the analysis on real linkage disequilibrium (1000 Genomes GRCh38 European panel) exposed the source of the significance. The original instrument sets had been built by distance pruning within the cis window rather than by LD clumping, so highly correlated variants (|r| up to 0.99) had been counted as independent instruments. The effective number of independent instruments was close to one: ATXN2L 1.23, CAPRIN1 1.28, OGFOD1 1.09, DDX1 1.07, DDX25 1.02, PABPC4 1.44, RBPMS2 1.97, LARP1 2.32. After clumping at r² < 0.001 the nominal p values moved by many orders of magnitude — OGFOD1 from 1.7 × 10⁻⁷ to 0.118, RBPMS2 from 7.0 × 10⁻⁸ to 0.366, LARP1 from 1.7 × 10⁻⁴ to 0.385, PABPC4 from 2.0 × 10⁻⁴ to 0.734 (Fig. 4). Two pairs retained FDR significance (ATXN2L 6.7 × 10⁻⁶, CAPRIN1 0.037), but both reduced to a single effective instrument — a Wald ratio rather than an IVW estimate — which is precisely the situation in which an MR estimate is not evidence of causality.

Colocalisation, computed on the same data, agreed with the corrected MR and not with the original. Across 46 gene–tissue tests the maximum posterior probability of a shared causal variant (PP.H4) was 0.108, and no test exceeded 0.5; the dominant hypothesis in 37 tests was H3 (two distinct causal variants) and in 9 was H1 (signal in one trait only) (Fig. 4c). An MR analysis that reports nine causal genes and a colocalisation analysis of the same loci that reports none is self-contradictory: the apparent signal is the repeated counting of one variant per locus.

**A generalisable diagnostic.** Whenever a cis-MR instrument set is built by selecting the k variants closest to the gene or by p-value ranking without LD-aware thinning, the effective instrument count should be reported alongside the estimate. In our data the nominal p value overstates significance by 2–26 orders of magnitude, and the direction of the overstatement is always towards more significance.

### 2.4 A design-clean negative: the gut–brain bridge

Not every null in this project was explained by data structure, and the contrast is instructive. We tested whether the SGRG panel could serve as a shared molecular bridge between UC and major depressive disorder (MDD), using a genome-wide MDD cohort (GSE19738; 66 MDD vs 66 controls, a design with adequate power) and four brain-region cohorts. Across a 13,835-gene universe in which 678 genes were differentially expressed in MDD and 90 of 104 panel genes were measured, panel enrichment was absent (OR = 1.14, p = 0.45, 55.1st percentile of the null); UC and MDD panel-wide effect sizes were uncorrelated (Spearman ρ = −0.068, p = 0.53; 41 of 88 genes concordant in sign); and the three-way intersection of UC DE genes, MDD DE genes and the panel contained **zero** genes. Six brain-region replications showed no systematic direction (2/5 to 5/5 concordant per region).

This is a negative result that the audit ladder passes at every rung: the controls are the study's own, the design is balanced, and the positive controls (the MDD DE genes themselves) are abundant. It therefore supports the *absence* of a panel-level shared bridge, in contrast to the other three analyses, which support only the *absence of testability*.

### 2.5 Rung 1 as a filter: how much public data can support this class of claim?

If the ladder is valid, it should predict which datasets can test the claim before any analysis is run. We therefore applied it as a whole-database filter for the specific question "is the SGRG panel differently expressed in inflamed versus uninflamed UC mucosa?", with four gates: assay measures mRNA; dataset contains UC; design provides at least six donor-paired or well-balanced arms; tissue is in vivo mucosa.

Of 397 CELLxGENE Discover collections, only 3 contain UC (12 datasets), and one provides a controlled single-cell resource (6 vs 6 donors, 100% primary cells — dataset ds1). Of 521 human inflammatory bowel disease series in GEO, 376 measure mRNA, 130 are single-cell or spatial, 13 contain any donor pairing, 4 contain six or more pairs, and **none** contains fifteen or more; no spatial dataset provides same-segment pairing. Applying all four gates left two qualifying resources: ds1 (already tested and negative under the calibrated null) and **GSE133060**, a bulk array study with 8 paired inflamed/non-inflamed UC biopsies that had never been analysed for this question. A qualifying resource that is found but not used invites the objection that the enumeration was decorative, so we analysed it (§2.6, Fig. 9–10). Separately, of 160 keyword-retrieved UC cohorts only 12 survived verification as usable two-arm datasets, with the largest single loss at MeSH confirmation, where 78% of PMID-carrying cohorts were not confirmed as UC — a useful reminder that the size of a literature-derived evidence pool is an optimistic upper bound, not an analysable sample.

### 2.6 The one powered design: a calibrated weak positive

The ladder is designed to say "this data cannot answer the question", and a framework that only ever says no is of little use. GSE133060 is the test of whether it can also say yes. The study deposits, for each of 8 UC patients, two mucosal biopsies — inflamed and non-inflamed — profiled on a whole-genome Agilent array (GPL4133; 19,749 genes after probe aggregation; 91 of 104 panel genes measurable). Because the contrast is within-patient and within-batch, the confounds that pervade the other levels are absent by construction.

The design has power. Five Hallmark inflammation programmes — inflammatory response, TNFA signalling via NFKB, interferon-gamma response, hypoxia and unfolded-protein response — rose in **8 of 8 patients** (paired Δz 0.27–0.82, Wilcoxon p = 0.0078, the minimum attainable for 8 pairs; Fig. 10b). This is the first time in the project that a known-positive control has recovered significance under any contrast; at every single-cell rung the same controls were non-significant in all cell types. The GSE133060 design can therefore detect an inflammatory signal that the atlas designs could not.

Yet the panel's ordinary p-value remains uninformative, and this is the sharpest calibration lesson in the paper. SGRG scored Δz = +0.208 with 7 of 8 patients higher in inflamed mucosa, giving a nominal paired Wilcoxon p = 0.0156 — which the template would report as a positive. But **250 of 500 expression-matched random gene sets (50%) were also nominally significant** under the identical test. The reason is a strong global axis: inflammation dilutes the non-immune transcriptome so systematically that the genome-wide mean Δz is −0.101, making "≥7 of 8 patients move together" the rule rather than the exception. Nominal p is inflated roughly ten-fold.

Calibrating against the matched null resolves it. The panel ranks **1st of 501** (panel plus 500 random sets), i.e. no random set reached its value (one-sided empirical p = 0.002). Two qualifications follow. First, the null distribution is asymmetric — 74 random sets reached an equal magnitude in the opposite direction — so the two-sided empirical p is 0.15, and only a pre-registered direction (the panel was hypothesised to rise in inflammation) licenses the one-sided reading. Second, removing the genome-wide axis restores two-sided significance (Δz = +0.309, two-sided p = 0.006).

The effect, however, is small: Δz = 0.21 is about three quarters of the weakest positive control (hypoxia, 0.27) and one quarter of the strongest (interferon-gamma, 0.82). The defensible summary is a **weak, directionally consistent, calibration-dependent positive** — not the strong, robust signature a template analysis of the same data would have reported. It leaves the single-cell conclusion untouched (those data remain structurally unable to test the claim) while removing the two objections most likely to be raised against this paper: that the ladder is a universal no, and that the one testable resource was left untested.

### 2.7 Rung 1 at library scale: auditability is confounded with quality

The ds4 finding — 94.4% of "control" cells re-used from external donors, discovered by reading a single column of a single dataset's metadata — raises a library-level question: how often is such a check even *possible*? We therefore read `cell_count`, `primary_cell_count` and the disease annotation of all 2,238 datasets in all 397 CELLxGENE Discover collections (292.8M cells; no h5ad download required) and classified every dataset by its disease labels: control-only, disease-only, or **mixed** (both control and disease labels in the same dataset).

Three numbers describe the library. First, re-use is the norm at cell level: **41.3%** of all cells (37.5% of human cells) are not primary to the dataset that reports them, and **36.3%** of datasets consist entirely of re-used cells. Second — and this is the number that matters for gene-set testing — the datasets that actually carry a case/control contrast are among the least attributable: of 346 mixed datasets (15.5% of the library), the median fraction of primary cells is **0.06**, and half of the human ones (49.8%) consist entirely of re-used cells. Third, arm-level auditing is almost never possible: of the 140 collections that contain a case/control contrast, only **15 (10.7%)** deposit the two arms as separate datasets; in the other 125 (**89.3%**) the contrast lives inside mixed datasets, whose cells cannot be assigned to arms from metadata at all.

The 15 auditable collections are not a random subset, and they are not uniformly clean. Their median control-arm re-use fraction is 0.00 — controls are usually the study's own when the study deposits them separately — but the range runs from 0 to 1.0, and in one collection the control arm is *entirely* re-used while the case arm is entirely primary (asymmetry = 1.0, the ds4 pathology in its purest form); 4 of 15 show control-worse asymmetry. Auditability is therefore confounded with quality in both directions: the subset we can check is enriched for clean controls, and for the 89.3% we must say "cannot audit", not "clean". The provenance check is thus not a routine gate but a privilege — the datasets that most need it are the ones it cannot reach. The control/case depth ratio in the auditable subset has median 0.86 (n = 14), a mild disadvantage compared with the 4.35× produced by ds4's borrowed controls.

The pattern is not a gut-specific artefact (Fig. 11c). Across tissue systems with at least five contrast-bearing collections, the share of collections whose arms cannot be separated from metadata ranges from 67% (kidney/urinary) to 100% (gut/intestine): blood/immune 91% (n = 47), brain/CNS 97% (n = 30), lung/airway 91% (n = 22), liver/biliary 80% (n = 15). Every major tissue system in the library behaves the same way.

### 2.8 Validation of the audit ladder on independent published claims

Nothing in the ladder is specific to stress granules or to colitis, so we tested whether it generalises by applying the identical protocol (§6.4 of the audit protocol) to two further published gene-set × disease claims. Both were drawn from an independent candidate pool, **frozen in registered tables before any analysis was run** (case A on 2026-10-10 at 23:12; case B at 20:38), and chosen to share no disease, tissue, data modality or gene-set family with SGRG × UC. The two cases deliberately exercise different rungs: case A the selection-bias/global-axis layer and a pseudoreplication defect; case B the provenance, depth and power layers.

**Case A: ferroptosis × hepatocellular carcinoma — reproducible but non-specific.** Yi et al. reported an 11-gene ferroptosis-related panel separating hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) from adjacent non-tumour liver with **AUC = 1.000** [28]. We rebuilt the pipeline with the paper's own logic (Student's *t* at p < 0.001 with no FDR correction, then LASSO ∩ SVM-RFE, then logistic ROC evaluated on the same samples) on an independent, larger cohort (GSE36376; 433 samples — 240 tumour, 193 adjacent non-tumour; 62 of the 64 `WP_FERROPTOSIS` genes measurable). The pipeline **reproduced**: 44 differentially expressed genes, in-sample AUC **0.9991** (Fig. 12a). The claim is therefore not a reporting error; the question becomes what produces AUC ≈ 1.

Its perfect performance is a property of the protocol rather than of the gene set. Five hundred size-matched random gene sets gave an in-sample median AUC of **0.9978**, and **100% of them reached ≥ 0.99**; under honest 7-fold cross-validation the panel scored 0.9898 against a random-set median of 0.9905, an empirical **p = 0.611** (Fig. 12a). No individual gene was remotely diagnostic (median single-gene AUC 0.680; maximum 0.953), yet 44 such genes reached AUC ≈ 1 — a jump that cannot originate in the genes themselves. The mechanism is a single global axis: PC1 separated tumour from adjacent liver with AUC **0.967**, the panel score correlated with genome-wide mean expression at ρ = **0.938**, and **removing PC1 collapsed the panel from 0.9991 to 0.757** while cutting the DE set from 44 to 18. A second, independent defect emerged in the paper's own discovery cohort (GSE65372): its 54 samples come from only **18 patients**, with **39 tumour samples from 17 patients**, yet the paper analyses them as independent observations — whereas the depositor's own series summary states the samples are "from the same patients". Aggregating to patient level inflated the effective sample size by **2.29×** and cut the p < 0.001 gene set from **8 to 2** (only TXNRD1 shared), but left AUC almost unchanged (0.9795 → 0.9847) — the same lesson as the PC1 analysis, reached independently. Finally, 9 of the 10 markers the paper lists are not ferroptosis genes (only HSPB1 is in the curated set) and the gene set was never released, so the panel's "ferroptosis" attribution cannot be independently verified.

**Case B: pyroptosis × Parkinson's disease — not recoverable from public data under the stated weights.** Wang et al. reported a two-gene pyroptosis score (POLR2K, TIMM8B) discriminating Parkinson's disease (PD) with **AUC = 0.823** (external validation 0.706) [29]. Using the published weights exactly as stated — score = −0.029 × POLR2K − 0.018 × TIMM8B — on publicly curated expression data (five bulk cohorts; 100 training samples, 59 PD / 41 control; 23 validation samples in GSE49036), we **could not recover the reported performance**: the training AUC was **0.6432** (reported 0.823) and the validation AUC **0.6250** (reported 0.706). We bound this statement precisely. The original processed and standardised matrices were not released and the raw data could not be retrieved, so our finding is that *the reported AUC cannot be recovered from public curated data using the paper's stated fixed weights* — **not** that the reported result is erroneous. Single-gene AUCs are invariant to monotone transformation but not to batch correction; if the gain came from an unreleased normalisation step, that step itself operates on an axis confounded with disease (below).

The direction, by contrast, did replicate: POLR2K was lower in PD in **5 of 5** cohorts and TIMM8B in 4 of 5; pooling the 100 training samples, the Mann–Whitney p was 0.143 (POLR2K) and 0.046 (TIMM8B), so neither gene reached significance on its own. The failure is in amplitude, not in sign. Three rungs localise it. *Rung 3:* the score was governed by dataset membership — ρ(score, genome-wide mean) = **−0.691** across pooled samples but only **−0.164** within datasets, so most of the score's variance is "which cohort is this sample from"; the genome-wide mean alone discriminated poorly (AUC 0.443 train, 0.500 validation), i.e. the technical axis dominated the score without itself classifying. *Rung 2:* the pooled training AUC (0.643) was **below the single-cohort AUC of three of the four training cohorts** (0.700, 0.722, 0.717; the largest cohort, GSE7621, was 0.486) — the signature of disease-unrelated between-cohort variance — and the training cohorts were all annotated `BATCH_CORRECTED_SUCCESS` while the validation cohort was annotated `BATCH_EFFECT_FAILURE`, so the two sides were processed under different batch regimes. *Rung 4:* against 500 random two-gene pairs the panel (in-sample AUC 0.6362) gave empirical **p = 0.285** (random median 0.6011, 95th percentile 0.7362) — a two-gene panel is indistinguishable from an arbitrary two-gene pair. *Rungs 1 and 5:* the single-cell "validation" arm was fully nested in the dataset — all **5 cases came from one dataset** (GSE157783) while **21 of 27 "healthy" controls came from datasets containing no case** (Cramér's V = **0.595**, χ² = 11.3, p = 0.0035) — and one of those datasets (GSE204796) is an *in vitro* hPSC-derived midbrain dopaminergic culture rather than human brain tissue; a five-case arm cannot support a diagnostic-performance claim. The gene set was also circularly defined: the "pyroptosis-related genes" were the DESeq2 output of a contrast between cells pre-sorted by their own pyroptosis-activity score (3,088 genes at p < 0.05), with no curated pyroptosis set cited.

At library scale, case B also tested whether the Rung-1 result is gut-specific. Of **356 human neurodegenerative datasets** in CELLxGENE (73.6M cells), **38.79%** of cells were non-primary, **48.6%** of datasets consisted entirely of re-used cells, and among the 100 contrast-bearing (mixed) datasets the median primary-cell fraction was **0.000** with **68%** fully re-used — a *more* severe provenance regime than the UC library (0.06, 50%; §2.7). The Rung-1 pathology is therefore not a property of one tissue.

**What the two cases establish.** Table 3 places the cases side by side. Three points follow. First, *the two panels failed for different reasons*: case A was reproducible but non-specific — AUC ≈ 1 was an emergent property of selecting 44 genes and scoring them in-sample, carried by one global axis, with pseudoreplication as an independent overlay — whereas case B could not be recovered from public data at all and carried a nested-control design defect. Second, *the decisive rung differed between the cases* (mechanism and pseudoreplication in A; provenance, depth and power in B), so the ladder is not a single test in five guises but five independently diagnostic checks. Third — and most important for interpretation — *in both cases the panel was statistically indistinguishable from random gene sets of the same size* (empirical p = 0.611 and 0.285), even though one reproduced its published number and the other did not. The common denominator is neither the gene set, the disease nor the platform, but the design and the data structure — exactly the objects the ladder audits. These two results answer the two objections a testability audit invites: that it is a universal "no" (case A shows it correctly refuses to credit a reproducible-but-non-specific claim), and that its failures are an artefact of one gene set (they reproduce in HCC and in Parkinson's disease).

## 3. Discussion

Our central claim is narrow and, we think, hard to dispute: in this project every null was traceable to a specific, measurable property of the available data, and the properties in question are checkable before the analysis is run. None of the five rungs is a novel statistical technique: batch confounding [21], technical-load confounding and normalisation artefacts [16,22], invalid permutation nulls [17], pseudoreplication [16] and post-selection inference [27] are all well documented. What we contribute is (i) an ordering of them by cost, so that the cheapest checks come first; (ii) an explicit statement of the failure signature for each, so that a failing dataset is identifiable from output rather than from intuition; and (iii) evidence that in a real, published-looking claim all five failure modes were simultaneously active and each was individually sufficient to produce the reported result.

The ladder also changes what a negative result means. Our SGRG × UC analyses are not evidence that stress granules are uninvolved in UC. They are evidence that the datasets we could access cannot answer the question: in the bulk cohorts because batch and disease are the same axis; in the single-cell atlas because the control arm was assembled from external donors at different depth; and in the summary statistics because the instruments were not independent. The defensible statement is not "SGRG is not a UC signature" but "the public resources currently available to test it do not permit a powered, provenance-controlled test". The one resource that does permit it (GSE133060) returned a weak, calibration-dependent positive (§2.6) — which is itself informative: the same data, read by the template, would have produced an over-confident claim.

Three implications follow.

**For authors.** A gene-set × disease paper should report five numbers alongside its result: the fraction of controls that are primary to the study; the batch–disease association and the batch-only AUC; the score–depth correlation and the depth ratio; the random-gene-set null for the test actually used; and the result of a known-positive control under the same design. All five are cheap. In our experience the third and fifth are the ones most often missing, and the ones most likely to reverse a conclusion: a panel that is significant in 13 of 26 cell types can be accompanied by a positive control that is significant in none.

**For database curators and platform maintainers.** Batch annotation exists and is already machine-readable in at least one major repository, but it is rarely surfaced at the point of use, and "batch-corrected" cohorts still frequently show a batch-only classifier AUC (median 0.568 in our data) that is not negligible. `is_primary_data` in CELLxGENE is a cell-level flag that is trivially checkable and is not currently part of any downstream workflow we observed — yet reading one column of metadata was enough to reverse the largest dataset's result.

**For the stress-granule question specifically.** The claim should be retested, not abandoned. Its testability condition is concrete: a mucosa dataset with balanced arms, ≥6 donor pairs, provenance-controlled controls, and at least one known-inflammatory positive control recovering significance. One such resource exists (GSE133060); tested here, it recovered the positive controls and returned a weak, directionally consistent panel signal that only emerged after calibration (§2.6). The correct summary of the literature is therefore that the SGRG–disease distribution reflects which diseases have been assayed and how, rather than which diseases the granule is involved in.

**Relation to precedent.** Recent work has audited different layers of the same problem. Skinnider et al. found that nearly half of deposited single-cell studies lacked the cell-level annotations required to reproduce their published analyses [12]; Sonrel et al. benchmarked the pipelines that produce them [13]; Huang et al. audited metadata completeness across tens of thousands of studies [14]; and Rogic et al. quantified whether single-cell data can be downloaded and re-used at all [15]. Those works ask whether data are *obtainable* and whether analyses are *reproducible*. We ask a third question: once data are obtained and the pipeline is reproduced, could the dataset have returned the answer it returned? That question is complementary, and unlike the others it is answered almost entirely from metadata and design properties.

**Validation on independent claims.** A testability framework is useful only if it returns the right verdict on claims it did not generate. We therefore applied the identical protocol to two further published gene-set × disease papers — ferroptosis × hepatocellular carcinoma and pyroptosis × Parkinson's disease — selected and frozen before any analysis (§2.8). Both claims failed the ladder, for different reasons, and in both the panel was indistinguishable from a random set of the same size: one was reproducible but non-specific, the other could not be recovered from public data under its own stated fixed weights. That the ladder is not tied to SGRG × UC is what licenses proposing it as a general reporting requirement rather than as a post hoc critique of a single literature.

## 4. Limitations

**Generalisation rests on two additional claims, not a systematic survey.** The ladder was developed on SGRG × UC and then applied to two further published claims (§2.8) — ferroptosis × HCC and pyroptosis × Parkinson's disease — chosen to differ in disease, tissue, data modality and gene-set family. That is enough to show the failure modes are not a property of one gene set, but it is not a survey: two cases, both anchored in bulk data, cannot establish base rates across the literature, and neither was a re-analysis of the original authors' own code. Whether the *frequency* of each failure mode in the published record resembles what we observed is an open, and separately answerable, question.

**The case-B non-reproducibility is bounded by an unreleased pipeline.** Our statement for case B (§2.8) is that the reported AUC cannot be recovered from public curated data using the paper's stated fixed weights — not that the reported result is erroneous. The original authors used processed, standardised matrices and a Harmony integration that were not released, and the raw single-cell data were not retrievable in our environment. We therefore cannot exclude that some unreleased preprocessing step reproduces the reported performance; if it does, that step operates on an axis confounded with disease, because the score is dominated by dataset membership (ρ = −0.691 pooled versus −0.164 within datasets).

**The one powered test is post-hoc selected and single-centre.** GSE133060 was identified by a database-wide search and is the only qualifying resource, so its result (§2.6) is not an independent replication; its one-sided significance depends on a pre-registered direction, and its effect size is a quarter of a known-inflammatory programme, on a single array platform from a single centre. It should be read as proof that the ladder can return a calibrated positive, not as a settled SGRG–UC finding.

**A calibration lesson that generalises beyond this panel.** Even in a design with demonstrable power, a nominal p = 0.016 was shared by half of the expression-matched random gene sets, and the single- versus two-sided empirical p differed by a factor of 75 because the null was asymmetric. Design power and p-value validity are therefore separate properties: a study can be powered and still report an inflated p, which makes random-gene-set calibration mandatory rather than optional whenever a gene set is scored against a genome-wide axis.

**Metric sensitivity of depth.** The case-to-control depth ratio is sensitive to the unit of analysis: at donor-pseudobulk level ds1 gives 0.86× and ds3 1.28×, whereas at cell level ds1 gives 1.52× and ds3 0.65× — the direction of the effect reverses. Depth ratios must therefore always be reported with their unit, and should not be treated as a load-bearing quantity on their own. The ds4 value (4.35× cell-level) is itself a consequence of the control-provenance problem, not an independent property of the disease.

**Metadata-level provenance auditing has a reach limit.** Our library-wide provenance scan covers all 397 CELLxGENE collections (2,238 datasets, 292.8M cells) but is bounded by what the metadata records. `frac_primary` is a dataset-level ratio; it cannot tell which *cells* within a mixed dataset are borrowed, nor whether a borrowed control dataset was itself re-processed. Most importantly, auditability is confounded with quality (§2.7): only 10.7% of contrast-bearing collections have separable arms, and these are enriched for clean controls, so the library-wide control-reuse figures we can compute describe the favourable subset, not the library. The 89.3% we cannot audit are recorded as "cannot audit", never as "clean".

**Keyword-based attribution.** The feasibility enumeration relies on keyword retrieval of disease and tissue, without MeSH verification or donor-level de-duplication. Published audits suggest keyword-based disease attribution fails at roughly the 30% level [14]; our own verification of 160 UC cohorts retained only 12 analysable two-arm datasets. All resource counts should be read as optimistic upper bounds.

**One MR pair retains nominal significance.** ATXN2L remains significant after LD clumping, but reduces to a single effective instrument and is not supported by colocalisation (PP.H4 = 0.003, PP.H3 = 0.997). We treat it as an uninformative estimate rather than a causal finding, and note that a single-variant cis-MR design cannot be strengthened by adding correlated variants.

## 5. Methods

**Gene set.** SGRG = union of GO:0010494 (cytoplasmic stress granule) and GO:0035061 (nuclear stress granule), retrieved from QuickGO for *Homo sapiens*: 156 symbols, 104 HGNC-approved after validation (52 rejected as withdrawn, non-coding or non-human). 97 genes were measured on the discovery array.

**Bulk transcriptomics.** Discovery GSE38713 / Gemma:5873 (43 human colonic mucosal biopsies; 15 UC active inflamed, 8 UC remission, 7 UC active non-inflamed, 13 controls) [20]. Main contrast: UC active inflamed vs control. Welch t-test on log₂ expression, Benjamini–Hochberg FDR within the panel. Batch was defined from the sample scan-date field; batch–disease association was measured with Cramér's V and a χ² test; batch-only and panel discriminability with logistic regression and AUC. Panel specificity was assessed against 200 random gene sets of identical size drawn from the 18,630-gene measured pool (L2-penalised logistic regression, C = 0.01) [27]. Batch centring subtracted the batch mean per gene — the simple alternative to model-based empirical-Bayes batch adjustment [21]. Validation cohort GSE11223 / Gemma:8385 (61 UC inflamed vs 62 normal colonic biopsies) [20].

**Cohort landscape.** 160 public UC cohorts retrieved from Gemma with machine-readable curation metadata (`batchEffect`, `batchEffectStatistics`, `batchConfound`, `geeq.publicQualityScore`) [20]. Batch separability and disease separability were computed from the cohorts' own expression matrices where available (n = 61 with both), using the leading principal components. Group comparisons used Mann–Whitney tests; all reported medians and differences are of cohort-level values.

**Mendelian randomisation.** Two-sample cis-MR, exposures from GTEx v8 cis-eQTLs (whole blood, colon sigmoid, colon transverse, brain cortex) [23], outcome from the de Lange 2017 UC GWAS (GCST004133; 12,366 cases / 33,609 controls; harmonised, GRCh38). IVW, weighted median, weighted mode and MR-Egger were computed. LD audit used the 1000 Genomes high-coverage GRCh38 phased panel (3,202 samples; European subset), with greedy clumping by eQTL p value at r² thresholds 0.001, 0.01 and 0.1, and the effective number of independent instruments estimated from the LD matrix. Colocalisation used `coloc.abf` [19] with the full cis-window summary statistics, and the MR and colocalisation evidence was read jointly as recommended by ref [18].

**Single-cell.** Four public UC mucosal scRNA-seq datasets [25,26,30,31], retrieved from CELLxGENE Discover [24]. Pseudobulk expression was computed per donor × cell type from raw counts — the unit of analysis recommended for cross-condition single-cell comparisons [16,17]; cell types required ≥20 cells per donor and ≥3 donors per group. Counts were library-size normalised and log-transformed following the standard variance-stabilising rationale [22]; gene-set scores were then computed with a scanpy-style control-gene-corrected score. Because scores are linear in the gene set, the real panel, the positive controls and 200 random sets were evaluated in a single matrix product. Random sets were drawn matched to the SGRG expression distribution and excluded all panel genes. Depth diagnostics comprised ρ(score, nUMI) within cell type, R²(log nUMI ~ disease), and the case/control depth ratio at donor and cell level. Confounding interventions comprised (i) down-sampling every cell to 1,000 UMI with the random sets carried through the same procedure, (ii) restriction to primary (study-original) controls. Tests were unpaired Mann–Whitney on donor-level pseudobulk, with a donor-label permutation test as a comparison; the minimum attainable two-sided p for a paired sign-flip test on n pairs is 2/2ⁿ.

**Provenance.** `is_primary_data`, `donor_id`, `cell_count`, `primary_cell_count` and dataset-level disease annotation were read from all 397 CELLxGENE collection-detail endpoints [24] (cached; no h5ad download). `frac_primary = primary_cell_count / cell_count`. Every dataset was classified by its disease labels into control-only (all labels match a control lexicon), disease-only, or mixed (both) — a mixed dataset carries its case/control contrast inside one dataset, so its arms cannot be separated from metadata. Arm-level re-use fractions, asymmetry and the control/case depth ratio (weighted `mean_genes_per_cell`, restricted to datasets with `frac_primary ≈ 1`) were computed only for collections with separable arms.

**Resource feasibility.** CELLxGENE Discover [24]: all 397 collections enumerated, filtered on disease annotation and control-arm availability. GEO: 521 human inflammatory bowel disease × gut-segment series retrieved with four complementary esearch queries; assay modality was taken from the `!Series_type` SOFT field (not from the abstract — one series with 43 same-donor pairs was described as spatial but measured miRNA); donor pairing was parsed from sample titles; pairing was rejected when case and control samples came from different gut segments.

**Powered bulk test (GSE133060).** Agilent two-colour FE files for 16 samples (8 patients × inflamed/non-inflamed) were parsed on the `gProcessedSignal` and `gIsWellAboveBG` columns and mapped to gene symbols via the GPL4133 annotation shipped with the series. Probes mapping to the same symbol were averaged (median), values were log₂-transformed, quantile-normalised across the 16 arrays and z-scored per gene across samples. Gene-set scores were the mean z over detectable set members; the paired statistic was a Wilcoxon signed-rank test on the 8 within-patient differences, with the direction fixed a priori (inflamed > non-inflamed). Calibration used 500 random gene sets matched to the SGRG expression distribution (excluding all panel genes); the empirical one-sided p is (1 + #{random ≥ panel}) / 501, with the two-sided and global-axis-removed variants also reported. Positive controls were five MSigDB Hallmark inflammation programmes.

**Software.** Python 3.13 (numpy, scipy, pandas, scikit-learn, h5py), R 4.6 (coloc 5.2.3, susieR 0.14.2). Figures were produced with matplotlib and R base graphics.

**Figure 1 legend (final).** *The five-rung testability audit ladder.* The upper band shows the template being audited — a curated gene set intersected with disease DE genes, prioritised by a supervised learner and reported as a biomarker panel — which never asks whether the dataset could have returned a negative. The ladder asks five questions, ordered by the cost of the check, each paired with the quantity to report and the signature of failure. Passing all five rungs licenses a calibrated test of the claim; failing any rung means the data cannot answer the question, which is a statement about the data rather than about the biology. The footer records the two independent published claims on which the unchanged ladder was validated (§2.8).
